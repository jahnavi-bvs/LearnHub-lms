# course: Introduction to Genetics

## module: Introduction to Genetics and Heredity
video: 2reevjoVn6c

### Introduction
Genetics is the study of how traits pass from parents to offspring and why individuals differ. It explains everything from eye colour to many inherited diseases.

### Key concepts
- **Heredity:** the passing of traits between generations.
- **Variation:** differences among individuals.
- **Genes and environment:** traits can be influenced by both.
- **Genotype and phenotype:** genetic makeup versus observable traits.

### Definitions
- **Gene:** a section of DNA that codes for a product.
- **Allele:** a version of a gene.
- **Trait:** a characteristic, such as height.
- **Genome:** an organism's complete DNA.

### Example
Height is strongly influenced by many genes, but nutrition during childhood also matters. So two people with similar genes can reach different heights.

### Key takeaways
- You inherit half your DNA from each parent.
- Phenotype results from genes interacting with the environment.
- Variation is what makes evolution possible.

## module: Cell Structure and Chromosomes
video: BbA-pHyirzo

### Introduction
Genetic information is stored inside the cell and packaged into chromosomes. This packaging lets very long DNA molecules fit into a tiny nucleus and divide accurately.

### Key concepts
- **Nucleus:** holds DNA in eukaryotic cells.
- **Chromosome structure:** DNA wrapped around histone proteins.
- **Human chromosomes:** 23 pairs, or 46 in total.
- **Mitosis:** makes identical body cells.
- **Meiosis:** makes sex cells with half the chromosomes.

### Definitions
- **Chromatin:** DNA and protein in the nucleus.
- **Centromere:** the point joining two sister chromatids.
- **Homologous chromosomes:** a matching pair, one from each parent.
- **Karyotype:** an image of a cell's chromosomes.

### Example
Sex chromosomes form the 23rd pair: XX is typically female and XY typically male. Each egg carries an X, and each sperm carries an X or a Y.

### Key takeaways
- Body cells are diploid (46); sex cells are haploid (23).
- Fertilization restores the full chromosome count.
- Meiosis shuffles genes and so creates variation.

## module: DNA and RNA Structure
video: JQByjprj_mA

### Introduction
DNA and RNA are the molecules of genetic information. Their structure explains how information is stored, copied and used.

### Key concepts
- **Nucleotides:** a sugar, a phosphate and a base.
- **DNA:** a double helix with bases A, T, G and C.
- **Base pairing:** A with T, and G with C.
- **RNA:** single-stranded, using U in place of T.
- **Types of RNA:** mRNA, tRNA and rRNA.

### Definitions
- **Nucleotide:** the building block of nucleic acids.
- **Double helix:** the twisted-ladder shape of DNA.
- **Complementary strands:** strands that match by base pairing.
- **Deoxyribose:** the sugar in DNA.

### Example
If one DNA strand reads `ATGC`, its complementary strand reads `TACG`. An RNA copy of the strand `ATGC` reads `UACG`, because RNA uses U in place of T.

### Key takeaways
- DNA stores information, while RNA helps to use it.
- Base pairing allows accurate copying.
- Hydrogen bonds hold the two DNA strands together.

## module: DNA Replication and Protein Synthesis
video: 6gUY5NoX1Lk

### Introduction
Cells must copy DNA before dividing, and they read it to build proteins. This flow of information, from DNA to RNA to protein, is called the central dogma.

### Key concepts
- **Replication:** copies DNA in a semi-conservative way.
- **Transcription:** copies a gene into mRNA in the nucleus.
- **Translation:** ribosomes build a protein from mRNA.
- **Codons:** three-base sequences that specify amino acids.

### Definitions
- **DNA polymerase:** the enzyme that builds new DNA strands.
- **Helicase:** the enzyme that unwinds DNA.
- **RNA polymerase:** the enzyme that makes RNA.
- **Codon:** three bases coding for one amino acid.

### Example
The mRNA codon `AUG` codes for methionine and also signals the start of a protein. A tRNA carrying methionine pairs with it, and the ribosome adds the following amino acids.

### Key takeaways
- Each new DNA molecule keeps one original strand.
- Transcription occurs in the nucleus, and translation in the cytoplasm.
- Proteins carry out most of the work in a cell.

## module: Mendelian Genetics and Inheritance
video: 3f_eisNPpnc

### Introduction
Gregor Mendel's pea plant experiments revealed the basic rules of inheritance. His findings still let us predict how traits are passed on.

### Key concepts
- **Law of segregation:** allele pairs separate when sex cells form.
- **Law of independent assortment:** different genes are inherited independently.
- **Dominant and recessive alleles:** dominant alleles mask recessive ones.
- **Punnett squares:** predict the offspring's chances.

### Definitions
- **Homozygous:** two identical alleles.
- **Heterozygous:** two different alleles.
- **Genotype:** the allele combination, such as `Tt`.
- **Phenotype:** the visible trait, such as tall.

### Example
Crossing two `Tt` tall plants gives:
```text
TT : Tt : tt = 1 : 2 : 1
Tall : Short  = 3 : 1
```
Each offspring has a 25% chance of being short.

### Key takeaways
- A recessive trait shows only when both alleles are recessive.
- Punnett squares give probabilities, not guarantees.
- Many human traits involve multiple genes and don't follow simple ratios.

## module: Mutations and Genetic Disorders
video: vl6Vlf2thvI

### Introduction
A mutation is a change in DNA. Most have little effect, and some are harmful, but mutations are also the source of genetic variation.

### Key concepts
- **Point mutations:** a single base changes.
- **Frameshift mutations:** adding or removing bases shifts the reading frame.
- **Chromosomal mutations:** large sections or whole chromosomes change.
- **Causes:** copying errors, radiation and certain chemicals.
- **Inheritance:** germline mutations can pass to children; somatic ones don't.

### Definitions
- **Mutation:** a change in DNA sequence.
- **Mutagen:** something that causes mutations.
- **Germline cell:** a sex cell or its precursor.
- **Carrier:** someone with one copy of a recessive disease allele.

### Example
Sickle cell disease is caused by a single base change in the haemoglobin gene. Down syndrome results from an extra copy of chromosome 21, which is a chromosomal change.

### Key takeaways
- Not all mutations are harmful.
- Carriers often have no symptoms but can pass the allele on.
- Genetic counselling helps families understand their risks.

## module: Modern Genetics and Genetic Engineering
video: CDw4WPng2iE

### Introduction
Scientists can now read and edit DNA directly. These tools are transforming medicine and agriculture while raising serious ethical questions.

### Key concepts
- **Recombinant DNA:** combining DNA from different sources.
- **PCR:** makes millions of copies of a DNA region.
- **CRISPR-Cas9:** a precise gene-editing tool.
- **Gene therapy:** treats disease by changing genes.
- **DNA sequencing:** reads the order of bases.

### Definitions
- **Genetic engineering:** directly changing an organism's DNA.
- **Plasmid:** a small circular DNA in bacteria, used to carry genes.
- **GMO:** a genetically modified organism.
- **Restriction enzyme:** cuts DNA at specific sequences.

### Example
Human insulin is produced by placing the human insulin gene into bacteria using a plasmid. The bacteria multiply and make insulin, which is then purified and used to treat diabetes.

### Key takeaways
- Genetic engineering already produces medicines and improved crops.
- CRISPR makes editing cheaper and more precise.
- Society must consider safety, privacy and fairness.
