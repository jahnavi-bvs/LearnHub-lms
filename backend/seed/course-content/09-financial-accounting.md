## module: Introduction to Financial Accounting
video: Rpa_UAciIeU

### Introduction
Financial accounting records a business's transactions and reports its performance to people outside the company, such as investors and lenders.

### Key concepts
- **The accounting process:** identify, record, classify, summarize and report.
- **Users:** investors, lenders, tax authorities and managers.
- **Main statements:** income statement, balance sheet and cash flow statement.
- **Financial vs management accounting:** outside users versus internal decision makers.

### Definitions
- **Asset:** something the business owns or controls that has value.
- **Liability:** an amount the business owes.
- **Equity:** the owners' claim after liabilities.
- **Revenue:** income from normal business activity.

### Example
A shop sells goods for ₹10,000 that cost ₹6,000, and pays ₹1,000 rent. Its profit is ₹3,000. Accounting records each transaction so that this result can be reported and trusted.

### Key takeaways
- Accounting is often called the language of business.
- Reports must be reliable and comparable.
- Profit and cash are not the same thing.

## module: Accounting Principles and Concepts
video: yYX4bvQSqbo

### Introduction
Accounting follows shared rules so that one company's reports can be compared with another's. These are set out in frameworks such as GAAP and IFRS.

### Key concepts
- **Business entity:** the business is separate from its owner.
- **Going concern:** assume the business will continue operating.
- **Accrual:** record items when earned or incurred, not when cash moves.
- **Matching:** record expenses in the same period as related revenue.
- **Consistency and prudence:** use the same methods, and don't overstate results.

### Definitions
- **GAAP:** Generally Accepted Accounting Principles.
- **IFRS:** International Financial Reporting Standards.
- **Accrual basis:** recording when the transaction occurs.
- **Materiality:** whether an item is large enough to affect decisions.

### Example
A firm completes work in March and gets paid in April. On the accrual basis, the revenue belongs to March, even though the cash arrives in April.

### Key takeaways
- Keep personal and business finances separate.
- Accrual gives a truer picture of performance than cash timing.
- Consistent methods make results comparable across years.

## module: Accounting Equation and Double Entry System
video: kwA_6MV8dFE

### Introduction
All of accounting rests on one equation. Double entry keeps it balanced by recording each transaction in two places.

### Key concepts
- **Accounting equation:** Assets = Liabilities + Equity.
- **Double entry:** every transaction has equal debits and credits.
- **Debit rules:** increase assets and expenses.
- **Credit rules:** increase liabilities, equity and revenue.

### Definitions
- **Debit (Dr):** the left side of an account.
- **Credit (Cr):** the right side of an account.
- **Account:** a record of one item, such as Cash.
- **Capital:** money invested by the owner.

### Example
An owner invests ₹50,000 in cash.
- Cash (an asset) increases by ₹50,000: debit Cash.
- Capital (equity) increases by ₹50,000: credit Capital.

Assets of 50,000 = Liabilities of 0 + Equity of 50,000. The equation balances.

### Key takeaways
- Total debits must always equal total credits.
- Debit and credit mean left and right, not good and bad.
- Every transaction affects at least two accounts.

## module: Journal Entries and Ledger Posting
video: J7ntz3YSzY0

### Introduction
Transactions are first recorded in date order in the journal, and then transferred to individual accounts in the ledger. This gives both a timeline and a running balance for each account.

### Key concepts
- **Journal:** the book of original entry, in date order.
- **Ledger:** groups all entries by account.
- **Posting:** moving journal entries to the ledger.
- **Balancing:** finding each account's closing balance.

### Definitions
- **Journal entry:** a record of the accounts debited and credited.
- **Narration:** a short description of the transaction.
- **T-account:** a simple form showing debits on the left and credits on the right.

### Example
Bought furniture for ₹20,000 in cash.
```text
Furniture A/c   Dr   20,000
    To Cash A/c            20,000
(Being furniture purchased for cash)
```
This is then posted to the Furniture and Cash ledger accounts.

### Key takeaways
- The journal shows what happened, and when.
- The ledger shows the total position of each account.
- Clear narrations make records easy to check later.

## module: Trial Balance and Rectification of Errors
video: w3hhhQxHiwo

### Introduction
A trial balance lists all ledger balances to check that debits equal credits. It catches many mistakes, but not all of them.

### Key concepts
- **Trial balance:** a statement of all account balances at a date.
- **Errors it reveals:** one-sided entries or wrong totals.
- **Errors it misses:** omitted transactions, wrong accounts, or compensating errors.
- **Rectification:** correcting errors with new entries, not by erasing.

### Definitions
- **Error of omission:** a transaction not recorded at all.
- **Error of commission:** a wrong amount or the wrong person's account.
- **Error of principle:** treating an item in the wrong category.
- **Suspense account:** temporarily holds a difference until it's found.

### Example
A ₹5,000 machine purchase is recorded as a repair expense. Debits still equal credits, so the trial balance agrees. This error of principle is corrected by debiting Machinery and crediting Repairs.

### Key takeaways
- An agreed trial balance doesn't prove the books are correct.
- Correct errors with entries, so there's a clear record.
- Prepare a trial balance before the final accounts.

## module: Depreciation and Valuation of Assets
video: _pas1ETbrj8

### Introduction
Long-term assets wear out or become outdated. Depreciation spreads an asset's cost over the years it is used, which matches expense to the revenue it helps earn.

### Key concepts
- **Causes:** wear, passage of time and obsolescence.
- **Straight-line method:** the same charge each year.
- **Written-down value method:** a fixed percentage of the remaining value.
- **Book value:** cost minus accumulated depreciation.

### Definitions
- **Depreciation:** allocating an asset's cost over its useful life.
- **Salvage value:** the estimated value at the end of its life.
- **Useful life:** how long the asset is expected to be used.

### Example
A machine costs ₹1,00,000, with a salvage value of ₹10,000 and a 5-year life.
```text
Annual depreciation = (1,00,000 - 10,000) / 5 = ₹18,000
```
After two years, its book value is ₹64,000.

### Key takeaways
- Depreciation allocates cost; it isn't a cash payment.
- Land is generally not depreciated.
- Use a consistent method from year to year.

## module: Preparation of Final Accounts
video: 4N0Dmzhi3Tw

### Introduction
At the end of a period, ledger balances are turned into reports that show profit and financial position. These are known as the final accounts.

### Key concepts
- **Trading account:** finds gross profit.
- **Profit and loss account:** finds net profit.
- **Balance sheet:** shows assets, liabilities and capital on a date.
- **Adjustments:** closing stock, outstanding and prepaid expenses, depreciation.

### Definitions
- **Gross profit:** sales minus the cost of goods sold.
- **Net profit:** gross profit minus other expenses.
- **Closing stock:** unsold goods at period end.
- **Outstanding expense:** an expense incurred but not yet paid.

### Example
```text
Sales 5,00,000 - Cost of goods sold 3,00,000 = Gross profit 2,00,000
Gross profit 2,00,000 - Expenses 1,20,000    = Net profit 80,000
```
The ₹80,000 is added to capital on the balance sheet.

### Key takeaways
- The profit and loss account covers a period; the balance sheet shows one date.
- Adjustments ensure that each period carries its correct expenses.
- The balance sheet must balance.

## module: Cash Flow Statements and Financial Reporting
video: DiVPAjgmnj0

### Introduction
A company can report a profit and still run out of cash. The cash flow statement shows where cash really came from and where it went.

### Key concepts
- **Operating activities:** cash from day-to-day business.
- **Investing activities:** buying or selling long-term assets.
- **Financing activities:** loans, share issues and dividends.
- **Indirect method:** starts from net profit and adjusts for non-cash items.

### Definitions
- **Cash flow:** cash moving in or out.
- **Free cash flow:** operating cash minus capital spending.
- **Annual report:** a company's yearly financial report.
- **Non-cash expense:** such as depreciation, which reduces profit but not cash.

### Example
A firm has a net profit of ₹50,000 and depreciation of ₹10,000, while customers owe ₹20,000 more than last year. Operating cash flow = 50,000 + 10,000 - 20,000 = ₹40,000.

### Key takeaways
- Profit is an opinion shaped by rules; cash is a fact.
- Healthy companies generate cash from operations.
- Read all three statements together for the full picture.
